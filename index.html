Research Paper

Which search and engagement signals are most strongly associated with high search visibility — and how can these insights prioritize SEO optimization?

This paper studies content-level and engagement signals in a pseudonymized warehouse of 208,636 content items, testing which signals associate with two related outcomes: raw search visibility (average position) and click-through rate (CTR). A Random Forest model explains up to 25.5% of visibility variance and 21.5% of CTR variance — both well above naive baselines. Word count, content type, and scroll engagement emerge as the consistent top signals across both outcomes, while backlinks, search volume, competition, and CPC show little independent association with either. The findings are offered as decision-support for content prioritization, not causal proof.

01

Introduction & Problem Statement

Which search and engagement signals are most strongly associated with high search visibility, and how can these insights help prioritize SEO optimization?

Content teams manage far more pages than they can manually optimize. Given a limited editing budget, which properties of a page — its length, type, or on-page engagement — are worth auditing first? This paper treats that as a measurable question rather than a matter of intuition: it separates two related but distinct outcomes — visibility (how well a page ranks, measured by average search position) and conversion of that visibility (CTR, how often an impression becomes a click) — and asks which signals predict each.

Decision this supports Prioritizing which content properties a team should review or test first, out of a large library, using observed associations rather than uniform across-the-board effort.
02

Data

Release: FlyRank Internship Pseudonymized Warehouse, version v20260703 — a frozen snapshot exported 2026-07-03. All client, content, and query identifiers are salted, namespaced, fingerprinted hashes; no raw client names, domains, URLs, or queries appear anywhere in this release.

Tables used:

  • dim_content (519,606 rows) — content-level signals: search volume, competition, CPC, backlinks, content type, main intent, word count, last-optimized date.
  • fact_content_daily_performance_sample.parquet — the latest full month, aggregated to one row per content item: impressions, clicks, average position, engaged sessions, scroll events.

Date window: confirmed directly from the data (not assumed from documentation) — 2026-06-01 through 2026-06-30.

Excluded, and why:

  • dim_clients — dropped after joining; the analysis is content-level, not client-level.
  • fact_content_query_90d — out of scope for this lane; better suited to query/CTR-specific analyses.
  • Full 78.8M-row daily history — a single representative month is sufficient for signal-association analysis and avoids unnecessary re-aggregation cost.
  • Content items with zero recorded GSC impressions (200,569 of 409,205 aggregated rows, ~49%) — these have no measurable CTR/position and would bias the model if imputed rather than excluded. This excluded group is a distinct problem, addressed separately in Recommendations.
Known limitation, from the source dataset This is an unbalanced panel — per-client GSC/GA4 history depth differs — so June 2026 is not equally represented across all clients in this warehouse.
03

Methodology

Assumptions: content-level signals (search volume, competition, backlinks, freshness) are treated as properties of a page going into the window, not outcomes of that month's performance — this keeps the analysis from being circular.

Labels (two outcomes modeled):

  • ctr = total_clicks / total_impressions, aggregated per content item.
  • avg_position = mean GSC average position per content item — a direct visibility measure.

Features: search_volume, competition, cpc, backlinks, content_type, main_intent, word_count (from dim_content); avg_position, engaged_sessions, scroll_events (aggregated, from fact_content_daily_performance). Skewed numeric features log-transformed; categoricals one-hot encoded.

Baseline: linear regression predicting CTR from average position alone — the simplest defensible bar, since position is the best-known standalone CTR driver.

Model: Random Forest Regressor (scikit-learn), run in three configurations: all signals → CTR, content/engagement signals only (position excluded) → CTR, and content/engagement signals → avg_position directly.

Validation: random 80/20 content-level split (42,728 held-out test items).

Correction from the original plan A time-aware split was originally planned, but the performance table was already aggregated to one row per content item per month before modeling — leaving no day-level time axis to split on. The actual split used is a random content-level split. Noted here rather than silently revised.

Leakage checks: confirmed avg_position is a separate GSC metric, not derived from CTR; confirmed no content item appears in both train and test; excluded any same-window metric that could only be known after the fact relative to the label being predicted.

The position-only linear baseline explains almost none of CTR's variance (R² = 0.011) — the position–CTR relationship is not well captured by a straight line. The full Random Forest roughly doubles this to 0.215, and even with position excluded, content/engagement signals alone still explain 0.133 — showing they carry value independent of ranking position. Notably, the same signals predict visibility itself (position, R² = 0.255) even better than they predict CTR without position — content-level signals appear more tied to getting found than to converting that visibility into clicks.

05

Limitations & Honest Framing

Association, not causationThe model identifies signals that move together with CTR and position — it does not establish that changing them causes visibility or clicks to change.
Most variance unexplainedR² peaks at 0.255. CTR and position remain driven by factors this dataset can't observe: query intent, SERP features, brand recognition, title/meta quality.
Single-month snapshotThe planned time-aware split wasn't used, since the data was already aggregated to one row per item per month — the actual split is a random content-level split. The model speaks to one cross-section, not trends over time.
Unbalanced panelPer-client GSC/GA4 history depth differs, so June 2026 isn't equally represented across all clients in the warehouse.
Roughly half the content excluded~49% of content items had zero recorded impressions and were dropped — this analysis says nothing about why that half isn't visible at all.
Uneven category sizescontent_type_feedly article shows high importance despite being a much smaller category than keyword article — a caveat, not a confident generalizable finding.
No algorithmic claimsNothing here claims to reveal or reverse-engineer any search engine's ranking algorithm — only correlational patterns in this dataset's own recorded outcomes.
06

Ranked Recommendations

Ordered by strength of observed association across both outcomes. Framed as decision-support — testable hypotheses to prioritize, not guaranteed outcomes.

01

Review and expand thin content

word_count is the top driver of visibility (61.5% importance) and the top content signal for CTR once position is excluded (45%). Highest-confidence, most actionable lever identified.

High confidence
02

Prioritize by content type, not uniformly

content_type shows strong independent association with both visibility (14.7%) and CTR (31%). Audit performance by content type before applying blanket changes across the library.

High confidence
03

Treat scroll/engagement as a feedback loop, not a lever yet

Scroll events correlate with better position (−0.187) and CTR, but engagement happens after a click — this may reflect well-ranked content attracting engagement, not the reverse. Flagged for follow-up research.

Needs follow-up
04

Treat search volume and competition as context, not levers

Higher search volume associates with worse position (+0.123), consistent with high-volume keywords being more competitive. Useful for interpretation, not something a content team can directly change.

Context only
05

Don't treat position as a standalone target

Position dominates CTR feature importance (~83%) but a position-only model barely explains CTR alone (R² = 0.011). Pair ranking efforts with content-quality signals above.

High confidence
06

Deprioritize backlinks and CPC as CTR/visibility levers

Both show under 5% importance across every model version in this analysis — lower priority relative to content-level signals for these two outcomes specifically.

High confidence
07

Separately audit the ~49% of invisible content

This analysis only covers content with measurable visibility. The excluded half of the library is a distinct, likely higher-impact problem worth its own investigation.

Next study
